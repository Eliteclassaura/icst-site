# Cooling tower resources

## What is here

Reference material for people who specify, operate and maintain field-erected cooling towers: material comparisons, glossaries, primers and engineering charts, alongside the technical articles.

## Material comparisons

The structural material decides maintenance load, service life and fire behaviour more than any other single choice, so the comparisons are kept as charts rather than prose.

**Wood.** Most components on early cooling towers, including the structure, were naturally decay-resistant wood. Wood is economical, widely available and a renewable resource. It is treated to improve longevity but remains susceptible to decay and to fire.

**FRP.** Inert and inorganic, chemically resistant, rot and decay resistant, close to maintenance free, and lighter — which lowers transportation cost. It takes surface treatments, is stronger than wood for its weight, and flexes to carry heavy loads, storms and ice, which allows a tighter tower design. Pultruded FRP is not inherently fire retardant: where the duty calls for it, a fire-retardant resin system is specified and the laminate is qualified to a flame-spread rating.

**Concrete.** Higher initial cost in most markets, and whether it is economic depends on the country, the location within it, and what else has to be imported. It offers longevity, is non-combustible, suits harsh tower environments, works with modern construction techniques, and is low maintenance.

## Tower types and draft configurations

The difference between the two is where the air meets the water and how the water is distributed, and that decides the trade-off.

**Counterflow** towers move air vertically upward against the falling water, and distribute water through a pressurised spray header above the fill. Available in induced and forced draft, in concrete, FRP and wood structure, with splash, film and low-fouling fills. The enclosed distribution system is not exposed to sunlight, which limits algae growth in the hot water side, and the plan area for a given duty is smaller. The spray header needs pump head to work, and the counterflow air path carries more static pressure drop, so both pump and fan power are generally higher than a crossflow of the same duty.

**Crossflow** towers move air horizontally across the falling water, and distribute water by gravity from open hot-water basins above the fill. That gravity distribution is the reason crossflow needs less pump head, and the shorter air path needs less fan power. The open basins are easier to reach for inspection and cleaning, and are also exposed to light, so they need a working treatment programme.

**Forced draft** towers put the fans at the base and push air through the tower, which puts the mechanical equipment in the cool, dry inlet air rather than in the hot saturated discharge — a consideration where the discharge is corrosive. The penalty is that the low-level inlet sits close to the discharge, so saturated air can recirculate back into it, and the inlet is more exposed to icing. **Induced draft** towers put the fans at the top and pull air upward against the falling water, which discharges at height and away from the inlet; this is the usual arrangement on heavy industrial duties.

**Natural draft** towers move air by convection and need no fan power at all. **Mechanical draft** towers move air with fans.
